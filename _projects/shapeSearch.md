When you search a kitchen for a tea kettle, you tend to check the stovetop first — a lifetime of experience has taught you where kettles usually appear. But because we enter the lab already carrying that experience, it has been difficult to study how this kind of attentional guidance is *acquired* in the first place.

This project asks a focused question:

> **Can attentional guidance emerge from statistical learning of object–location regularities in naturalistic scenes?**

To find out, I built a visual search task in which participants searched for one of four target shapes embedded within kitchen scenes. Each shape was probabilistically tied to a different scene region (e.g., floor, counter, wall), appearing in its associated region on 75% of trials. No participant was told about these regularities.

Across experiments, participants became reliably faster at locating targets in their high-probability regions than in low-probability regions — demonstrating that attentional guidance can emerge through statistical learning alone, even in complex real-world scenes.

**Key findings**

- Attentional guidance can be learned purely from object–location statistics, without explicit instruction.
- The learned guidance is expressed as faster search to high-probability scene regions.
- The effect generalizes across naturalistic scene contexts.

This work has been accepted for publication:

> **Frandsen, J. L.** & Anderson, B. A. (accepted). *The role of statistical learning in attentional guidance during search through naturalistic scenes*. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition.*
